1Core concepts

Digital images as functions

Big idea

A digital image is a function. Forget "picture" — think of it as a machine that takes a location and returns a value.

  • Domain = space — the coordinates you can plug in. A 2D photograph has a 2D grid domain; a 3D medical scan (CT/MRI volume) has a 3D grid domain.
  • Range = what comes out — scalar or vector. Black-and-white photo → scalar. Color photo → vector [R, G, B]. Video → a function of space and time.
Analogy

A digital image is like a spreadsheet of temperature readings across a city. Each cell (domain = lat/long grid) holds a temperature (range = scalar). A color photo is the same spreadsheet, except each cell holds three readings instead of one — like temperature, humidity, and wind speed all at once.

Colormaps are a paint-by-numbers lookup table — the same scalar data array can be visualized very differently depending on the colormap applied. The data never changes, only its translation into color.

RGB vs. multispectral vs. hyperspectral

TypeBandsAnalogy
RGB3Tasting a dish and noting sweet, sour, salty only
Multispectral~4–15Also noting bitter and umami
HyperspectralHundredsRunning a full chemical assay on the dish

A hyperspectral image is really a cube: two spatial dimensions plus a spectral dimension. Slicing at one wavelength gives an ordinary grayscale image; stacking all slices gives every pixel's full spectral fingerprint.

Brush-up — image basics
What are the domain and range of a color video?

Domain = space and time (2D pixel grid across a sequence of frames). Range = a vector, the RGB triplet at each space-time coordinate.

Does changing a colormap change the underlying image data?

No. A colormap is only a lookup table for visualizing scalar data — the stored values are untouched; only their color translation changes.

Why can hyperspectral imaging reveal things RGB cannot?

RGB collapses the whole visible spectrum into just 3 broad bands. Hyperspectral samples hundreds of narrow bands across the spectrum, capturing fine spectral signatures (e.g. material composition) that get averaged away in RGB.

2Image formation

The pinhole camera

Camera obscura — nature's first camera

In Latin, camera obscura means "dark chamber." It's a natural phenomenon: light rays passing through a tiny hole into a dark space form an image where they strike a surface — no lens required.

Why does the image flip?

A ray leaving the top of a tree outside travels in a straight line, passes through the pinhole, and — unable to bend — keeps going straight, landing near the bottom of the back wall. A ray from the bottom of the tree lands near the top. Result: the projection is inverted (up↔down) and reversed (left↔right).

This happens by accident in real buildings — a hole in a tiled roof at Prague Castle has projected the outside world onto an attic wall. Artists in the 1700s–1800s even carried portable camera-obscura tents into the field, tracing the projected (inverted) scene to sketch with perfect perspective.

The geometry: 3D world to 2D image

Set up the pinhole camera: the pinhole sits at the origin O = (0,0,0); the image plane sits a distance f (focal length, f > 0) behind it. A world point has coordinates (x1, x2, x3); its projection has 2D coordinates (y1, y2).

Analogy

The pinhole is a strict bouncer at a club door: every ray must pass through that one exact point to get "in." Once through, it travels straight until it hits the back wall. Because all rays funnel through one point, the geometry becomes pure similar-triangles trigonometry.

Using similar triangles, for a scene point with x3 > 0, the real (inverted) image plane gives:

y1 = -f · x1 / x3 y2 = -f · x2 / x3

Every point along the ray from O through P projects to this same (y1, y2) — an entire 3D ray collapses onto a single 2D point. This is perspective projection.

The virtual image plane

Working with inverted coordinates is annoying for analysis, so we place a virtual image plane a distance f in front of the pinhole instead of behind it. This flips the signs and avoids the inversion entirely during analysis:

y1 = f · x1 / x3 y2 = f · x2 / x3
Analogy

It's like designing a video-game camera. You could simulate light literally flipping onto a wall behind the lens — or symbolically place your "screen" in front of the camera and skip un-flipping everything later. Same math, friendlier bookkeeping.

A small but powerful detour: homogeneous coordinates

Ordinary (x, y) coordinates can't represent points at infinity — but perspective projection cares about exactly such points (parallel lines "meeting" far away, sunlight arriving as parallel rays).

The trick: represent a 2D point (x, y) as the whole family {(x·S : y·S : S) for any nonzero scale S}. To recover the point: given (p : q : r), the actual point is (p/r, q/r).

Take a line through the origin, points (bt, -at). Its homogeneous coordinates are (bt : -at : 1) ~ (b : -a : 1/t). As t → ∞, this becomes (b : -a : 0) — a well-defined coordinate for "infinity" that ordinary (x, y) could never express.

Analogy

It's like adding a "zoom level" dial (S) to your coordinate system. Zoom all the way out to S = 0 and you can still meaningfully talk about which direction you were heading — even though you never quite arrive.

Perspective projection properties

  1. Straight lines → straight lines. A planar surface in 3D, sliced by the image plane, produces a straight line segment in 2D.
  2. Spheres → ellipses (in general) — a ball photographed off-center doesn't project to a perfect circle.

Pinhole limitations — and why we use lenses

  • Small pinhole → sharp image, but very dark.
  • Large pinhole → brighter image, but blurry.

Real cameras replace the pinhole with a lens: lighter, sharp, and bright, because a lens gathers light from a wide area and still focuses it to a point. A thin, symmetric lens approximates pinhole geometry, but doesn't perfectly model geometric distortion or the fact that different colors refract slightly differently through glass.

Field of view

Field of view (FOV) is the angular extent of the world a camera can see. Human vision spans roughly 120° horizontally (binocular) by 60° vertically (central). As you zoom a lens in, FOV reduces.

Brush-up — pinhole camera
Why is the camera-obscura image inverted both vertically and horizontally?

Light travels in straight lines through the single pinhole. A ray from the top of the scene must cross to the bottom of the image plane to keep going straight, and a ray from the left crosses to the right — giving both a vertical and horizontal flip simultaneously.

What problem does the "virtual image plane" solve, and does it change the physics?

It doesn't change the physics at all — it's a bookkeeping convenience. Placing the plane symbolically in front of the pinhole flips the sign of y1 and y2, letting you work with positive, non-inverted coordinates during analysis.

Why do we need homogeneous coordinates at all for perspective projection?

Because ordinary Euclidean coordinates cannot represent "points at infinity" — and vanishing points, parallel rays, and points that recede to infinity are central to perspective geometry. Homogeneous coordinates give those points a well-defined algebraic representation.

3Perspective geometry

Vanishing points

Everyone has seen this: train tracks running toward the horizon appear to converge to a single point. Let's prove why.

Take a 3D line parametrized by t:

x1(t) = d1 + a·t x2(t) = d2 + b·t x3(t) = d3 + c·t

Its perspective projection:

y1(t) = (d1 + a·t) · f / (d3 + c·t) y2(t) = (d2 + b·t) · f / (d3 + c·t)

As t → ∞, the offsets (d1, d2, d3) — where the line starts — cancel out entirely:

y1(t) → a·f / c y2(t) → b·f / c (assuming c ≠ 0)
The punchline

Any line with direction (a, b, c) — regardless of where it starts in 3D — projects to the exact same vanishing point (af/c, bf/c). Only direction matters, never position.

Analogy

Two friends walk in exactly the same compass direction from different houses. From a satellite view directly above, their paths look parallel and never meet. From a low camera far ahead of them on the ground, both paths appear to converge to the same point on the horizon — because what matters to the 2D view is their shared direction, not their different starting positions.

Bonus proof — the projected path really is a straight line. Differentiating y1(t) and y2(t) shows the slope dy2/dy1 is independent of t — meaning the projected curve has constant slope everywhere, i.e. it's a straight line.

Special case: c = 0

When c = 0, the line is parallel to the image plane (x3 stays constant). Here there is no vanishing point — the projected slope equals b/a, the same as the 3D line's own slope, so parallel 3D lines project to parallel 2D lines that never meet (think of horizontal fence rails running left-right in front of you, staying parallel in the photo).

Brush-up — vanishing points
Do two parallel lines starting at very different 3D locations share the same vanishing point?

Yes — as long as they have the same direction (a, b, c). The vanishing point formula af/c, bf/c depends only on direction, never on the offsets d1, d2, d3.

Under what condition does a set of parallel 3D lines have no vanishing point at all?

When c = 0, i.e. the lines run parallel to the image plane itself. Then they project to parallel 2D lines that never converge.

What does it mean, geometrically, that dy2/dy1 is independent of t?

It means the slope of the projected curve is constant at every point along it — which is exactly the definition of a straight line. This is why perspective projection always maps 3D straight lines to 2D straight lines.

4Hardware

Image sensing & Bayer filters

From photons to numbers

Modern cameras use one of two sensor technologies, both built on MOSFET technology:

  • CCD — Charge-Coupled Device
  • CMOS — Active-Pixel Sensor

Both do the same job: convert photon hits → electric current → voltage, across a 2D array of light-sensitive elements. On a CCD specifically, the image is projected through a lens onto a capacitor array — each capacitor accumulates charge in direct proportion to the light intensity falling on it.

Analogy

Picture a grid of rain buckets left out in a storm. Each bucket (capacitor/pixel) collects rainwater (photons) for a fixed period. A bucket under a heavy downpour (bright light) fills far more than one under a drizzle (dim light). At the end, you measure the water level — that's your brightness value.

Color separation: the Bayer filter

A bare capacitor can't tell what color photon it absorbed — only how many. The fix: overlay a mosaic of tiny color filters, one per sensor element — a Bayer pattern (Bryce Bayer, Eastman Kodak, 1975). The most common layout is RGGB:

R G G B

repeated across the entire sensor.

Why twice as many green elements?

The pattern mimics human eye physiology — we're far more sensitive to green wavelengths, which sit closest to the peak of our luminance perception. Dedicating 50% of sensor elements to green captures the most perceptually important detail at the highest resolution.

Analogy

If you're staffing 4 quality inspectors and know "brightness defects" get noticed 60% of the time versus 20% each for "redness" and "blueness" defects, you'd staff 2 inspectors on brightness and 1 each on the rest — exactly what RGGB does for green vs. red/blue.

After capture, each sensor location knows only one of R, G, B. Two paths forward: (1) accept a lower-resolution RGB image directly, or (2) demosaic / de-Bayer — interpolate the two missing channels at every pixel from neighboring readings, producing full-resolution RGB.

Brush-up — sensing & Bayer
What physical quantity does a CCD capacitor actually accumulate, and what does it accumulate in proportion to?

It accumulates electric charge, in direct proportion to the light intensity (number of photons) falling on that capacitor.

Why can't a raw sensor element measure color directly, and what's the fix?

A capacitor only measures how much light hit it, not its wavelength/color. The fix is placing a mosaic of tiny colored filters (a Bayer pattern) over the sensor array, so each element only receives light already filtered to one color band.

In an RGGB Bayer pattern, what has to happen before you get a full-resolution RGB image?

Demosaicing (de-Bayering) — interpolating the two missing color channels at every pixel location using neighboring sensor readings.

5Discretization

Sampling vs. quantization

These two concepts get mixed up constantly — keep them strictly separate.

Spatial samplingIntensity quantization
What's limited?Where you measure (domain)What values a measurement can take (range)
CauseFinite number of sensor elementsFinite bits per pixel
Failure modeMoiré patterns, aliasingBanding, posterization

Spatial sampling

An image is stored at a finite set of discrete locations because memory is finite — you record one value per grid cell rather than at every infinitesimal point. There's a direct trade-off: for the same sensor chip and exposure time, packing in more sensor elements reduces the field-of-view each individual sensor covers ("per-pixel FOV"). More pixels = finer sampling, but weaker per-pixel signal, and tiny sensors are harder to manufacture.

Analogy

Sampling is like surveying a city's opinion by knocking on doors. If you knock on only every 10th house (undersampling), you might miss a pattern repeating every 7 houses entirely — or worse, misread it as some other, slower-changing pattern. That misreading is exactly what causes Moiré patterns.

Moiré patterns: undersampling a scene with fine, repetitive detail (a striped shirt, a chain-link fence) can't distinguish the true fine pattern from certain coarser patterns — a false, swirling interference pattern appears that was never in the scene.

Temporal analogy

Ever seen a spinning helicopter rotor look like it's moving slowly backward on video? That's the temporal version of the same undersampling problem — frame rate too slow to correctly capture true rotation speed.

Intensity quantization

After sampling fixes where you measure, quantization limits how many distinct values each measurement can be recorded as — literal rounding of brightness values to a limited set of allowed levels. With N bits, you get 2ᴺ possible intensity levels (8 bits → 256 levels, the classic 0–255 range).

Analogy

Quantization is like converting a continuous 0–100% exam score into a letter grade. Two students scoring 91% and 89% might land in different bins (A vs. B) even though their true performance was nearly identical — the rounding error inherent to quantization, entirely separate from how many students you tested (that's the sampling question).

Brush-up — sampling & quantization
A photo shows a swirling pattern that wasn't in the original scene. Is this a sampling or quantization problem?

Sampling — this is a Moiré pattern, caused by undersampling a scene with fine repetitive detail relative to the sensor's spatial sampling rate.

An image looks fine in detail but shows visible "banding" in a smooth sky gradient. Sampling or quantization?

Quantization — too few intensity levels (too few bits) means smooth gradients get rounded into visibly distinct steps/bands.

If you double the number of sensor elements on the same chip, what's the immediate trade-off?

Each sensor element covers a smaller per-pixel field of view, gathering less light and producing a weaker signal per pixel — a direct trade-off between sampling rate and signal strength.

6Color engineering

Color spaces

Additive (RGB) vs. subtractive (CMY)

RGB (additive)CMY (subtractive)
Starts fromBlack (no light)White (paper reflecting all light)
MechanismAdd colored lightSubtract (absorb) with ink
Used forScreens, TVs, camerasPrinting

RGB starts from total darkness and builds a color by adding red, green, and blue light. Colors on the outer surface of the RGB cube (where at least one channel is at max) are called saturated colors.

CMY works by absorbing light reflecting off a page:

Cyan absorbs red → C = B + G Magenta absorbs green → M = R + B Yellow absorbs blue → Y = R + G
C = 1 - R M = 1 - G Y = 1 - B

White = C=M=Y=0 (no ink). Black = C=M=Y=1 — in practice not perfectly black on real printers, hence the extra black-ink cartridge (K).

Analogy

RGB is like mixing colored spotlights on a dark stage — shine red, green, and blue together and you get white. CMY is like painting with colored filters over white paper in daylight — every filter can only block more light, never add any.

Luminance — why the weights are unequal

The naive gray value A = (R+G+B)/3 is perceptually wrong — humans don't perceive R, G, B as equally bright. We're most sensitive to green, then red, then blue. So we use a weighted sum instead:

Analog TV (Rec. 601): Y = 0.2990·R + 0.5870·G + 0.1140·B Digital devices (Rec. 709): Y = 0.2125·R + 0.7154·G + 0.0721·B
Analogy

Like a teacher weighting a final grade 20/30/50 across homework, midterm, final instead of a flat average — because the final exam is a better indicator of understanding. Here green is the "final exam" of color perception.

Desaturation

[Rd, Gd, Bd] = s·[R,G,B] + (1-s)·[A,A,A] where A = (R+G+B)/3

A convex combination of the original color and its gray equivalent. s between 0–1 → desaturation; s > 1 → increased saturation (clamped to valid range).

HSV & HSL — thinking like a painter

RGB is great for hardware but terrible for human intuition. Painters think in Hue (which color), Saturation (how vivid), Value/Lightness (how bright/dark).

  • HSV maps to a cylinder (or cone): central axis = grays, white at top, black at bottom. Pure saturated colors sit on the top rim, getting darker moving down the surface.
  • HSL maps to a double cone: white at the top point, black at the bottom point, maximally saturated colors bulging around the middle.
Painter's terminology

Tint = color + white (moving toward light — pastels). Shade = color + black (moving toward dark — deep, moody colors). Tone = color + gray (moving toward the neutral axis — muted, dusty colors).

YUV / YCbCr — engineering color for compression

These color spaces exist to separate luminance (Y) from two chroma components, because:

  1. Human vision is far more sensitive to detail in lightness than in color.
  2. It preserves compatibility with legacy black-and-white systems.
  3. It lets engineers allocate bandwidth/storage asymmetrically — more bits to Y, fewer to chroma.
YUV (analog TV, NTSC/PAL): Y = luminance U = scaled (B - Y) V = scaled (R - Y) YCbCr (digital TV, JPEG): wR = 0.299, wG = 0.587, wB = 0.114 Cb ≈ scaled (B - Y) Cr ≈ scaled (R - Y)

Y vs. Y′ (luma): Y (luminance) is technically from linear RGB. In practice, systems apply gamma correction to RGB first (R′G′B′), and the resulting quantity is properly called luma (Y′), a nonlinear function of Y — a distinction textbooks are often loose about.

Chroma subsampling

Since chroma detail matters less to our eyes, engineers throw away chroma resolution to save bandwidth. Notation 4:X:Y = CbCr samples per 4 luma samples across two rows:

  • 4:4:4 — full chroma resolution
  • 4:2:2 — half horizontal chroma resolution
  • 4:2:0 — half horizontal and vertical chroma resolution
Analogy

Describing a crowd photo over a slow phone line: you'd read out every person's exact silhouette/brightness (Y — full resolution, since sharp edges matter most for recognition) but only mention the general color zone every couple of people, since losing a little color precision is barely noticeable. That's the trade TV broadcast and JPEG make with 4:2:2 / 4:2:0.

Brush-up — color spaces
Why does cyan ink absorb red light specifically, rather than some other color?

Cyan is defined as the RGB combination of blue and green light (B + G) with no red. So cyan pigment is engineered to absorb exactly the red component of incoming white light, reflecting the blue and green back.

If you wanted to compress an image and could only keep full resolution in one "channel," which would you keep — luminance or chroma — and why?

Luminance (Y). Human vision is far more sensitive to detail in brightness than in color, so keeping Y at full resolution and subsampling the chroma channels (as in 4:2:2 or 4:2:0) loses little perceptible quality while saving significant bandwidth/storage.

What's the geometric difference between how HSV and HSL represent "pure, fully saturated" colors?

In HSV (cylinder/cone), pure saturated colors sit on the top outer rim, at maximum Value. In HSL (double cone), pure saturated colors bulge out around the middle, at Lightness = 0.5 — with both white and black as separate points at the top and bottom.

8Answer key

Worked solutions

◐

ATTEMPT ALL THREE PROBLEMS BEFORE EXPANDING ANY SOLUTION BELOW

Solution 1 — Pinhole aperture size reveal

(a) Brightness: Increasing the pinhole size increases brightness. A bigger hole lets more total light rays pass through per unit time onto any given point of the sensor, so more photons accumulate → higher signal → brighter image.

(b) Sharpness: Increasing the pinhole size decreases sharpness. With a tiny/ideal pinhole, essentially only one ray from each scene point reaches any given point on the image plane — a clean, sharp mapping. As the hole grows, many slightly different rays from the same scene point pass through different parts of the opening and land on slightly different points on the image plane. These overlapping contributions blur what should have been one crisp point into a small blob.

Takeaway: pinhole geometry cannot deliver both maximum brightness and maximum sharpness — exactly why real cameras use a lens, which gathers light from a wide opening while still focusing rays back down to a sharp point.

Solution 2 — Perspective projection reveal

Given:

f = 5 cm (x1, x2, x3) = (12 cm, 6 cm, 24 cm)

Step 1 — y1:

y1 = f · x1 / x3 = 5 · 12 / 24 = 60 / 24 = 2.5 cm

Step 2 — y2:

y2 = f · x2 / x3 = 5 · 6 / 24 = 30 / 24 = 1.25 cm

Final answer:

(y1, y2) = (2.5 cm, 1.25 cm)

Sanity check: the scene point has x1 twice as large as x2 (12 vs 6), and the projection preserves that ratio (2.5 vs 1.25) — as expected, since perspective projection uniformly scales both in-plane coordinates by the same factor f/x3.

Solution 3 — Luminance & CMY reveal

Given: R = 0.80, G = 0.40, B = 0.20

Part (a) — Luminance:

Y = 0.2990·0.80 + 0.5870·0.40 + 0.1140·0.20 = 0.2392 + 0.2348 + 0.0228 = 0.4968

Y ≈ 0.497 — roughly mid-gray, since R and G are moderately-to-fairly bright while B is dim, and green's dominant weight (0.587) pulls the result up despite G being smaller than R.

Part (b) — CMY conversion:

C = 1 - 0.80 = 0.20 M = 1 - 0.40 = 0.60 Y_ink = 1 - 0.20 = 0.80

Final answer: Y (luminance) ≈ 0.497, (C, M, Y_ink) = (0.20, 0.60, 0.80)

Interpretation: the original color is a strong orange (high R, medium G, low B — think terracotta). Very little cyan is needed (C = 0.20, since R is high), but a lot of yellow ink is needed (Y_ink = 0.80, since B is low) — exactly what you'd expect for printing orange: mostly yellow, moderate magenta, little cyan.
